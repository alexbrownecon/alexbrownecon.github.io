About

My research predicts and explains human behavior in novel ways using the formalized framework of economic theory. The approach lies between two extremes: a fully empirical one that documents deviations from theory without explaining them, and a fully theoretical one that derives predictions without empirical verification. While I have written papers at either end of this spectrum, the ultimate goal is a unified theory of human behavior with the formality of economics.

My work requires most of the tools of economics: theory, empirics, experimental design, and an extensive knowledge of the literature in experimental methodology and decision making. My research often begins with formalized economic theory. I distill it down to make sharp predictions I can test in the lab. From there I follow the scientific approach: results either support an aspect of the theory or do not. These insights then inform further theory development. Applying behavioral economic theory outside of economics is also an aim. I have collaborated on federally sponsored projects to improve both traffic and electricity grids, as well as federal disclosure policies. Along those lines, a recent paper of mine, "Beyond Income: Dynamic Consumer Financial Vulnerability" (Journal of Marketing, 2023), was named a 2026 Distinguished Winner of the AMA-EBSCO-RRBM Award for Responsible Research in Marketing.

Research Themes

Equilibrium Selection, Game Theory & Mechanism Design

How do players coordinate on equilibria? When are mechanisms simple enough to work in practice? My work tests refinements of Nash equilibrium, studies strategic thinking in field settings like the movie industry, and examines auction design and contract mechanisms.

Decisions & Consumption

How do people make choices about risk, savings, and spending over time? This stream includes meta-analysis of loss aversion, dynamic savings with temptation, debt repayment behavior, capital allocation in firms, and preferences for the resolution of uncertainty.

Policy

Connecting experiments and behavioral insights to real-world policy — from the spillover effects of the CARD Act on student loans, to how nudges shape express lane choices, to measuring dynamic financial vulnerability of consumers.

Experimental Methods

Developing and testing experimental tools: when do separated elicitation methods improve measurement? What can 2,000 classroom bargaining experiments teach us about general economic principles? Which value elicitation mechanisms perform best?

TAMU Undergraduate Catalog →

I teach classes on experimental methodology, behavioral financial economics, and game theory. The courses are demanding: while some of the insights can be taught quickly, a full understanding requires considerable quantitative work.

My classes also include optional and additional materials so that students can develop their research agenda. I have worked with such students at the undergraduate, MS, and PhD levels, and several have become coauthors. My own undergraduate research produced my first paper, Brown and Kagel (2009), and set the direction of my career. (Prospective students should know that I supervise research only for students enrolled at Texas A&M.)
