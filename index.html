About

My research predicts and explains human behavior in novel ways using the formalized framework of economic theory. The approach lies between two extremes: a fully empirical one that documents deviations from theory without explaining them, and a fully theoretical one that derives predictions without empirical verification. While I have written papers at either end of this spectrum, the ultimate goal is a unified theory of human behavior with the formality of economics.

My work requires most of the tools of economics: theory, empirics, experimental design, and an extensive knowledge of the literature in experimental methodology and decision making. My research often begins with formalized economic theory. I distill it down to make sharp predictions I can test in the lab. From there I follow the scientific approach: results either support an aspect of the theory or do not. These insights then inform further theory development. Applying behavioral economic theory outside of economics is also an aim. I have collaborated on federally sponsored projects to improve both traffic and electricity grids, as well as federal disclosure policies. Along those lines, a recent paper of mine on consumer financial vulnerability won an award for responsible research from the American Marketing Association.

Research Themes

Behavioral Decision Making and Theory

These papers study choices made under uncertainty, over time, or both, answering questions about the robustness of loss aversion, how dynamic savings decisions falter under temptation, behavioral strategies for debt repayment, capital allocation in firms, and preferences for the resolution of uncertainty.

Equilibrium Feasibility, Selection, and Mechanism Design

My work examines under what conditions the equilibrium solution is a useful predictor of human behavior. Applications include estimating non-equilibrium models of limited strategic thinking in field settings and testing whether auctions and mechanisms perform as designed.

Policy Applications

Several projects connect experimental and behavioral results to policy. Examples include the spillover effects of the CARD Act on student loan borrowing, informing drivers for more efficient traffic grid utilization, and measuring and addressing consumer financial vulnerability.

Experimental Methods

Studies in this area develop and test the tools of experimental economics itself: how well our elicitation techniques actually measure underlying subject preferences. One paper finds consistent results in bargaining and trade from 2,000 classroom experiments.
